1.4 Schrödinger Equation: Differential Equation for Matter Wave

A classical wave is represented by a partial differential equation:

$$\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2}=v^2\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}\tag{1.4(i)}$$1‑Dimensional
$$\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2}=v^2\nabla^2y\tag{1.4(ii)}$$3‑Dimensional

In the above 2 equations: $y$ represents the disturbance of the wave. $v$ represents the velocity of the wave.

Similarly the matter wave as suggested by De‑Broglie should also be represented by a differential equation. This differential equation was suggested by Erwin Schrödinger in the year 1925. This equation is the starting point of quantum mechanics. It may also be noted that this equation is the most basic equation of Quantum Mechanics and it cannot be derived from anything else. However Schrödinger arrived at this equation by taking various inputs from De‑Broglie hypothesis and Planck’s Quantized Energy. Let us try to see the motivation and logical arguments behind writing down the Schrödinger equation.

(i) 1‑d Harmonic Progressive wave propagating along x‑axis can be written as:

$$\psi=Ae^{i(Kx-\omega t)}\tag{1.4(iii)}$$

$\psi$ represents the disturbance. $A$ → Amplitude; $K$ → Wave Number, $K=\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}$; $\omega$ → Angular Frequency.

(ii) De‑Broglie Hypothesis: $\lambda=\dfrac hP$. ∴ $K=\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}=2\pi\times\dfrac Ph\Rightarrow$

$$K=\frac P\hbar\tag{1.4(iv)}$$

where $\hbar=\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$ → This is called Reduced Planck constant.

(iii) Energy of the particle is quantised just like photons: $E=h\nu$. Here $\nu$ is the frequency of the wave associated with the particle. ∴ Angular Frequency is given by: $\omega=2\pi\nu=2\pi\times\dfrac Eh\Rightarrow$

$$\omega=\frac E\hbar\tag{1.4(v)}$$

(iv) Substituting Eqns 1.4(iv) & 1.4(v) into eqn. 1.4(iii).

$$\psi=Ae^{\frac i\hbar(Px-Et)}\tag{1.4(vi)}$$

Eqn. 1.4(vi) represents equation of a matter wave of a particle having momentum $P$ and energy $E$.

(v) $\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=Ae^{\frac i\hbar(Px-Et)}\times\Big(-\dfrac{iE}{\hbar}\Big)=-\dfrac{iE}{\hbar}\psi\ \Rightarrow\ i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\dfrac{iE}{\hbar}\psi\times i\hbar\ \Rightarrow$

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=E\psi\tag{1.4(vii)}$$

⇓

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\equiv E\tag{1.4(viii)}$$Energy operator

This implies that the operation $i\hbar\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}$ on the wavefunction $\psi$ is equivalent to multiplying it with $E$. Thus the operator $i\hbar\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}$ is called the Energy operator.

(vi) $\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}=\dfrac{iP}{\hbar}Ae^{\frac i\hbar(Px-Et)}$ or $\dfrac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}=\dfrac{i^2P^2}{\hbar^2}Ae^{\frac i\hbar(Px-Et)}$

Now; $-i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}=-i\hbar\times\dfrac{iP}{\hbar}Ae^{\frac i\hbar(Px-Et)}=-i^2P\psi=P\psi$ ∴

$$-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\equiv P_x\tag{1.4(ix)}$$Momentum operator

(vii) For a Non‑Relativistic particle in a potential $V(x)$ the total mechanical energy can be written as:

$$E=\frac{P^2}{2m}+V(x)$$1‑Dimensional

In general this equation is valid for both conservative & Non‑conservative system. For a conservative system $E$ = conserved. For a Non‑conservative system $E$ is not conserved. Now multiply this energy equation with $\psi$ on both side.

∴ $E\psi=\dfrac{P^2\psi}{2m}+V(x)\psi$

(viii) Now if we replace the Energy & Momentum with its corresponding operator then we arrive at the Schrödinger Equation.

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi\tag{1.4(x)}$$

Eqn. 1.4(x) is the 1‑Dimensional Schrödinger Equation (Time Dependent). This equation is called Time dependent Schrödinger equation as because in the equation $\psi$ is a function of both $x$ & $t$. $\psi(x,t)$: Wavefunction of the Particle under potential $V(x)$.

Note: In general potential may depend on time. But for most of the cases we will consider time independent potential. Thus $V$ will be a function of $x$ only.
Note: The steps we used to arrive at the Schrödinger equation is not at all a rigorous derivation. It is just an establishment from logical arguments.
Note: The Time Dependent Schrödinger Equation is a partial differential equation & by solving it for various potentials we can get $\psi(x,t)$. The form of $\psi(x,t)$ we assumed for arriving at the Schrödinger equation is not the only solution. Eqn. 1.4(iii) is indeed a solution of the Schrödinger equation but that is only for a free particle i.e when $V(x)=0$.
Note: Two points that proves the Non‑Rigorousness of the Derivation of the Time Dependent Schrödinger Equation:

1. We assume a solution $\psi(x,t)=Ae^{i(Kx-\omega t)}$ and from there we try to arrive at the differential equation. This is not mathematically sound.

2. Replacing the $E$ & $P$ by its corresponding operator is not at all allowed in Classical Mechanics.

Thus Schrödinger Equation is one of the basic postulates of Quantum Mechanics and cannot be derived from anything else.

1.5 Basic Aim of Classical Mechanics

1. Let us assume a particle of mass $m$ moving in 1‑dimension.

2. First thing that we need to know are the Forces acting on the particle. Let the forces be $\{F_1(x,t);\,F_2(x,t);\,F_3(x,t)\}$.

3. Once we know the Forces we can write down the differential equation of the particle using Newton’s 2nd Law of motion:

$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=F_1(x,t)+F_2(x,t)+F_3(x,t)\tag{1.5(i)}$$

4. Next we want to solve this differential equation and calculate the position of the particle as a function of time. $\{x(t)\}$

5. The solution $x(t)$ may contain some arbitrary integration constants. These constants are estimated from given initial conditions. Eg: Let at $t=0\Rightarrow x(0)=x_0$ & $v(0)=v_0$.

6. Thus the Newtonian Classical mechanics tells us that if we are given the initial position and velocity of the particle at $t=0$; then we can determine the position of the particle & hence velocity at any later time $t$ using N2L.

Given $\begin{cases}x(0)=x_0\\v(0)=v_0\end{cases}\Rightarrow(\text{N2L})\Rightarrow x(t)=\dots,\ v(t)=\dfrac{dx}{dt}=\dots$

6. Once we get $x(t)$ we can estimate any other dynamical variable like:

(i) Velocity: $v(t)=\dfrac{dx}{dt}$

(ii) Acceleration: $a(t)=\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}$

(iii) Momentum: $P(t)=m\Big(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\Big)$ etc.

Eg 1: A particle executing Simple Harmonic Motion (S.H.M): Conservative system

Step 1: From Definition of S.H.M the only force acting on a particle executing SHM is the restoring force. ∴

$$F_R=-kx\tag{1.5(ii)}$$$x$ → Displacement of the particle

Step 2: Write Down the Differential Equation of motion using Newton’s 2nd law of motion:

$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx\tag{1.5(iii)}$$

Step 3: Solve this Differential equation and calculate displacement as a function of time:

$$x(t)=R\cos(\omega_0t-\Phi)\tag{1.5(iv)}$$

Here $R$ → Amplitude; $\omega_0$ → Angular frequency & $\Phi$ → Epoch for oscillation (Initial phase).

Step 4: In the above solution $R$ & $\Phi$ are arbitrary constants which has to be determined from initial conditions. Eg: If at $t=0\Rightarrow x(0)=0$ & $v(0)=v_0$. Then

$$\Phi=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}\quad\&\quad R=\frac{v_0}{(-1)^n\omega_0}\tag{1.5(v)}$$

By substituting these constants we can write down the full solution:

$$x(t)=\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\sin(\omega_0t)\tag{1.5(vi)}$$

Thus from this equation 1.5(vi) we can tell the position of the particle at any point of time.

The velocity can also be estimated at any point of time by differentiating eqn. 1.5(vi).

1.6 Basic Aim of Quantum Mechanics

1. Applicability of Quantum Mechanics:

(i) Consider an electron $e^-$ of $v=100$ m/s. $m_e=9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg.

De‑Broglie Wavelength:

$$\lambda=\frac{h}{m_ev}$$

∴ $P=(9.1\times10^{-31}\times100)$

$$P=9.1\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$$Momentum of the Electron

∴ $\lambda_e=\dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{9.1\times10^{-29}}\Rightarrow\lambda_e=0.7281\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m}\Rightarrow$

$$\lambda_e=7.281\ \mu\mathrm{m}$$

This wavelength lies in the infra‑red spectrum and this wavelength is very well in the detectable range.

(ii) Consider a cricket ball moving with velocity $v=100$ m/s & having mass $m=0.15$ kg. Momentum of the ball $=(0.15\times100)=15$ kg m/s.

De‑Broglie Wavelength: $\lambda=\dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{15}\Rightarrow$

$$\lambda=4.42\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm{m}$$

Size of nucleus is of the order of $10^{-15}$ m. Thus the de‑Broglie wavelength of a cricket ball is $10^{20}$ times smaller than the Nucleus. Such small wavelengths cannot be detected with the instruments that we have. Thus observing quantum mechanical effects for such small De‑Broglie wavelength is very difficult.

Quantum Mechanical effects are significant when size of the particles becomes comparable to the De‑Broglie wavelength of the particle.

$$\lambda\approx d$$$d$ → size of the Particle; $\lambda$ → De‑Broglie Wavelength

2. Let us consider a particle of mass $m$ moving in 1‑Dimension subjected to a potential $V(x)$.

3. In Quantum Mechanics our first aim is to write down the time dependent Schrödinger equation for this particle.

$$i\hbar\Big(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\Big)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi$$

where $\hbar=\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$ & $i=\sqrt{-1}$. The wavefunction $\psi$ is in general a function of $x$ & $t$. ∴ $\psi(x,t)$: Wavefunction of a particle.

4. Next we solve this Schrödinger Equation and estimate the wavefunction of the particle $\psi(x,t)$.

5. Then the arbitrary constants are estimated from initial conditions: $\psi(x,0)=$ Given

6. Thus if $\psi(x,0)=$ Given; then $\psi(x,t)$ i.e the wavefunction at a later time $t$ can be determined by solving Schrödinger Equation.

Thus Schrödinger equation plays exactly the same role in quantum mechanics that N2L plays in Classical Newtonian mechanics.

1.7 What is the Physical Significance of the Wavefunction ψ(x,t) of a Particle?

(i) $\psi(x,t)$ does not have any physical significance BUT, $|\psi(x,t)|^2$ has got physical significance.

$|\psi(x,t)|^2$ → Probability Density → Probability of finding the particle at point $x$, at a time $t$.

(ii) To be more precise:

$$|\psi(x,t)|^2dx=\psi^*(x,t)\,\psi(x,t)\,dx\tag{1.7(i)}$$

= Probability of finding the particle between point $x$ & $(x+dx)$.

Note: We are assuming a particle moving in 1‑Dimension i.e the particle is moving in a line.

Eqn. 1.7(i) represents the Probability of finding the particle in the shaded region of the figure (Between C & D).

(iii) Probability of finding the particle between A & B will be given by

$$\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\psi^*(x,t)\,\psi(x,t)\,dx$$

(iv) Analogical Example: Linear charge distribution:

If we have a non‑uniform linear charge distribution $\lambda(x)$ then we can calculate the total charge between O & L by integrating.

Charge between P & Q $=\lambda(x)\,dx$
Total charge between O to L $=\displaystyle\int_0^L\lambda(x)\,dx$
Similarly to calculate the Probability of finding the particle between two points we have to integrate the Probability density from one point to the other.

(v) Suppose we solve the Schrödinger Eqn. for a particle and we get a wavefunction $\psi(x,t)$. Then we plot $|\psi(x,t)|^2$ as a function of $x$ and get something like the figure shown below:

From this figure we can conclude that:

(a) Probability of finding the particle at point D is highest.

(b) Probability of finding the particle at point E is zero.

(c) Probability of finding the particle at point A is greater than that of point F.

(d) Area of the shaded region represents the Probability of finding the particle between points B & C $=\displaystyle\int_{x_2}^{x_3}|\psi|^2dx$.

(vi) In general $\psi(x,t)$ may be complex. $\psi^*$ → complex conjugate of $\psi$.

∴ If $\psi=a+ib$ then $\psi^*=a-ib$; $\psi^*\psi=(a^2+b^2)$

If $\psi=Re^{i\theta}$ then $\psi^*=Re^{-i\theta}$; $\psi^*\psi=R^2$

If $\psi=A$ (Real) then $\psi^*=A$ (same as $\psi$); $\psi^*\psi=A^2$

Thus $\psi$ may be complex BUT; $(\psi^*\psi)$ is always real quantity.

VERY IMPORTANT NOTE: In Quantum Mechanics the state of a particle or system is completely determined by the wavefunction of the particle $\psi(x,t)$. Thus once we estimate $\psi(x,t)$; any other dynamical variable of the particle can be determined from this wavefunction.

1.8 Basic Properties of Wavefunction

1. Normalization: If Physically $|\psi(x,t)|^2$ has to represent Probability density then the total probability has to be 1.

$$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx=1\tag{1.8(i)}$$

This condition is called Normalization condition.

* How to Normalize a wavefunction?

→ Important Property of Schrödinger Equation: If $\psi$ is a solution of Schrödinger Equation then $(A\psi)$ is also a solution of Schrödinger Eqn. where $A$ is a constant. Thus multiplying the wavefunction with a constant do not affect it.

Proof: Given $\psi$ is a soln of Schrödinger Eqn.

∴ $i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi$ : Given

We have to Prove that:

$$\underbrace{i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\{A\psi\}}_{\text{L.H.S}}=\underbrace{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\{A\psi\}+V(x)(A\psi)}_{\text{R.H.S}}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\text{L.H.S}&=i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\{A\psi\}=A\,i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\\&=A\Big\{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}(\psi)+V(x)\psi\Big\}=\underbrace{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\{A\psi\}+V(x)A\psi}_{\text{RHS}}\end{aligned}$$

Thus L.H.S = RHS is Proved.

Using this property we can Normalize a wavefunction by multiplying it with an appropriate constant.

Let for Eg: $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx=N$ (A finite $\neq1$ Number). Thus $\psi$ is not normalized. Then we define a new wavefunction:

$$\psi_{new}=\frac{\psi}{\sqrt N}\ ;\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{new}^*\,\psi_{new}\,dx=1$$

Thus $\psi_{new}$ is Normalized which we get by multiplying $\psi$ with a constant $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt N}$.

Q) When can we normalize a wavefunction and when we cannot?

ANS: If $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx$ = Finite then $\psi$ is Normalizable and $\psi$ is also called square integrable. BUT If $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx=\infty$ (undefined) then $\psi$ is neither Normalizable nor square integrable. Thus we can say that if $\psi$ is square integrable then it is also Normalizable.

Q) What is a square integrable function?

ANS: A function $f(x)$ is said to be

(i) Integrable if $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(x)\,dx$ = Finite

(ii) square integrable if $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|f(x)|^2dx$ = Finite

Thus from this definition we can conclude that for $|\psi|^2$ to represent Probability density: $\psi(x,t)$ may not be integrable BUT, it has to be square integrable.

1.8 Basic Properties of Wavefunction (continued)

2. The wavefunction of a particle must be finite, single valued and continuous at all points in space.

3. The first order derivative of $\psi$ w.r.t $x$ i.e $\Big(\dfrac{d\psi}{dx}\Big)$ must either be continuous or piece wise continuous.

A function is said to be piecewise continuous if it has a finite number of finite discontinuity.

Eg:

$f(x)$ is piecewise continuous.

As we can see $f(x)$ has got 5 discontinuities and the amount of discontinuities are also finite.
Points of Discontinuities $\{x_1,x_2,0,x_3,x_4\}$.

Important Points to be Noted regarding 2nd & 3rd properties of wavefunction

(i) The continuity of $\psi(x,t)$ at all points and piecewise continuity of $\Big(\dfrac{d\psi}{dx}\Big)$ at all points can be proved from the Schrödinger Equation itself.

(ii) The finiteness of $\psi(x,t)$ at all points in space indicates that the probability of finding the particle at any point must have a finite value. It may be noted that the wavefunction has to be finite even at infinity i.e at $x=\infty$ also $\psi(x,t)$ = Finite.

(iii) The single valuedness of $\psi(x,t)$ comes from the fact that the probability of finding the particle at one point must have 1 value only. Thus $\psi(x,t)$ cannot be multi‑valued at any point.

(iv) Now let us try to explain the 3 properties of the wavefunction $\psi(x,t)$ given in point 2 of Basic Properties of $\psi(x,t)$.

(a) Finite (at all points in space)

Eg 1:

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}ax&0\lt x\le1\\0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}$$

This function is finite at all points.

Eg 2: $f(x)=ax$ for $-\infty\lt x\lt\infty$

This function is infinite at $x=\infty$. Thus this function cannot be considered continuous at all points. Thus such a function cannot represent the wavefunction of a particle.

Edit note. Original: “Thus this function cannot be considered continious at all points.” Kept as written. Under the heading “Finite”, the actual reason it fails is that f(x) = ax is not finite at x = ∞.

(b) Single valued function at all points:

Eg 1: $f(x)=x^2$ for $-\infty\lt x\lt\infty$

This function is single valued at all points as for every $x$ we have only 1 value of $f(x)$. Although $f(2)=4$ & $f(-2)=4$; These 2 values of $f(x)$ for $x=2$ & $-2$ are same but that does not break the condition of single valuedness. As for 1 $x$ we have only 1 $f(x)$.

Eg 2: $f(x)=\sqrt x$ for $-\infty\lt x\lt\infty$

This is a multi‑valued function. For $x=4$; $f(x)=\{+2,-2\}$. For 1 value of $x$ we may have more than one value of $f(x)$.

(c) Continuity of a function (Boundaries):

Eg 1: $f(x)=ax^2$ for $0\lt x\le\infty$; $f(x)=0$ for $-\infty\lt x\le0$

This function is continuous at $x=0$ and also at all other points. $x=0$ is the Boundary.

Eg 2:

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}ax&0\lt x\le1\\0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}\qquad\text{Boundaries: }x=0\ \&\ x=1$$

This function is continuous at $x=0$; BUT discontinuous at $x=1$.

Thus the wavefunction representing a particle physically should have the following properties:

① $\psi(x,t)$ should be finite at all points in space including $x=\infty$.

② $\psi(x,t)$ should be single valued at all points in space.

③ $\psi(x,t)$ should be continuous at the boundaries.

④ $\psi(x,t)$ should be square integrable.

Thus if a wavefunction is given and you are asked to check whether it can represent the wavefunction of a particle or not; then you have to check for these 4 properties.

Problem 1

Let the wavefunction of a particle is given by $\psi(x,0)=Ae^{\kappa x}+Be^{-\kappa x}$ over all space. What conditions should $A$ & $B$ satisfy such that it can represent the wavefunction of a particle physically.

ANS:

(i) The wavefunction has to be finite at all points.

As $x\to\infty$; $\psi(x,0)$ must be finite. ∴ For $x\gt0$: $A=0\Rightarrow\psi_R(x,0)=Be^{-\kappa x}$

(ii) The wavefunction must be continuous at boundaries → $x=0$.

∴ $\psi_L(x=0)=\psi_R(x=0)\Rightarrow$

$$B=A$$

∴ The wavefunction becomes:

$$\begin{aligned}\psi_L(x,0)&=Ae^{\kappa x}&&\text{for }x\lt0\\\psi_R(x,0)&=Ae^{-\kappa x}&&\text{for }x\gt0\end{aligned}$$

1.9 Two Important Properties of Schrödinger Equation

① It can be proved from the Schrödinger Equation that

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx=0\ \Rightarrow\ \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx=\text{constant with time.}$$

Thus from here we can say that if $\psi(x,0)$ at $t=0$ is Normalized then it will remain normalized at any later time $t$ as it evolves according to Schrödinger equation.

If $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*(x,0)\,\psi(x,0)\,dx=1$ then at a later time $t$ we will also have:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*(x,t)\,\psi(x,t)\,dx=1$$

Thus we have to normalize the wavefunction only once.

② Principle of Superposition: If $\{\psi_1,\psi_2,\dots\psi_n\}$ are solutions of Schrödinger equation then their linear combination $\psi=C_1\psi_1+C_2\psi_2+\dots+C_n\psi_n=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_i\psi_i$ will also be a solution of the Schrödinger Equation.

Proof for 2 functions $\{\psi_1\ \&\ \psi_2\}$

Given: $\psi_1$ & $\psi_2$ are solutions of Schrödinger Equation.

To prove: $(C_1\psi_1+C_2\psi_2)$ is also a solution of Schrödinger Equation.

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Given: }\ i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi_1}{\partial t}&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi_1}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi_1\\i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi_2}{\partial t}&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi_2}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi_2\end{aligned}$$

To Prove:

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\{C_1\psi_1+C_2\psi_2\}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\{C_1\psi_1+C_2\psi_2\}+V(x)\{C_1\psi_1+C_2\psi_2\}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\text{L.H.S}&=i\hbar C_1\frac{\partial\psi_1}{\partial t}+i\hbar C_2\frac{\partial\psi_2}{\partial t}\\&=C_1\,i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi_1}{\partial t}+C_2\,i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi_2}{\partial t}\end{aligned}$$

From the Given eqn’s for $\psi_1$ & $\psi_2$:

$$\begin{aligned}&=C_1\Big\{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi_1}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi_1\Big\}+C_2\Big\{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi_2}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi_2\Big\}\\&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}(C_1\psi_1)-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}(C_2\psi_2)+V(x)C_1\psi_1+V(x)C_2\psi_2\\&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\{C_1\psi_1+C_2\psi_2\}+V(x)\{C_1\psi_1+C_2\psi_2\}\\&=\text{R.H.S}\qquad\text{(Proved)}\end{aligned}$$

Problem 2

Consider a wave function $\psi(x,t)=Ae^{\alpha x+i\beta t}$ where $A$, $\alpha$ & $\beta$ are real positive quantities. Can it represent the wave nature of a particle?

ANS: This wavefunction must satisfy the 4 conditions stated in section 1.8. First let us check the square integrable condition.

$$\begin{aligned}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx&=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}Ae^{\alpha x-i\beta t}\cdot Ae^{\alpha x+i\beta t}\,dx\\&=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}A^2e^{2\alpha x}\,dx=A^2\Big[\frac{e^{2\alpha x}}{2\alpha}\Big]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\\&=\frac{A^2}{2\alpha}\{e^{\infty}-e^{-\infty}\}=\frac{A^2}{2\alpha}\times\{\infty-0\}=\infty\end{aligned}$$

Thus we see that the wavefunction is not square integrable & hence not normalizable. Hence this function cannot represent the wavefunction of a particle.

Thus we cannot normalize it and calculate the value of constant $a$; as integration over $-\infty$ to $\infty$ is not possible without knowing the wavefunction over all region. $$\begin{aligned}&\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi^*\psi\,dx=1\\\Rightarrow\ &\int_0^1A^2x^2\,dx+\int_1^2A^2(2-x)^2\,dx=1\\\Rightarrow\ &A^2\frac{x^3}{3}\Big|_0^1+A^2\int_1^2(4+x^2-4x)\,dx=1\\\Rightarrow\ &\frac{A^2}{3}+A^2\Big\{4x+\frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{4x^2}{2}\Big\}\Big|_1^2=1\\\Rightarrow\ &\frac{A^2}{3}+A^2\Big\{8+\frac83-8-4-\frac13+2\Big\}=1\\\Rightarrow\ &\frac{A^2}{3}+A^2\Big\{\frac{14}{3}-\frac{13}{3}\Big\}=1\\\Rightarrow\ &\frac{A^2}{3}+\frac{A^2}{3}=1\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{2A^2}{3}=1\end{aligned}$$

$$A=\sqrt{\frac32}$$

Thus the wavefunction becomes:

$$\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,x&\text{for }0\le x\le1\\\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,(2-x)&\text{for }1\le x\le2\\0&\text{for elsewhere}\end{cases}$$

(iv) Probability of finding the particle between $x=0.8$ & $1.1$

$$\begin{aligned}&=\int_{0.8}^{1.1}\psi^*\psi\,dx=\int_{0.8}^{1}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,x\Big)^2dx+\int_1^{1.1}\Big\{\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,(2-x)\Big\}^2dx\\&=\frac32\int_{0.8}^{1}x^2\,dx+\frac32\int_1^{1.1}(4+x^2-4x)\,dx\\&=\frac32\times\frac{x^3}{3}\Big|_{0.8}^{1}+\frac32\Big[4x+\frac{x^3}{3}-2x^2\Big]_1^{1.1}\\&=\frac12\{1-(0.8)^3\}+\frac32\Big[4\times(1.1)+\frac{(1.1)^3}{3}-2(1.1)^2-4-\frac13+2\Big]\\&=\frac{1-(0.8)^3}{2}+\frac32\Big\{\Big(4.4+\frac{(1.1)^3}{3}+2\Big)-\Big(4+\frac13+2\times(1.1)^2\Big)\Big\}\end{aligned}$$
