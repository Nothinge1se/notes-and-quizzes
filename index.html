1. Introduction to Carpentry

Scope, core responsibilities, and modern material evolution

PDF Page 1 • Lines 5–21

Definition & Scope (Page 1, Lines 5–9): Carpentry is a skilled trade and art form centered on cutting, working, and joining timber. Its scope ranges from large-scale construction, such as buildings, to finer items like furniture and cabinets. Carpenters are essential in constructing, erecting, installing, and repairing residential and small commercial structures.

Material Evolution (Page 1, Lines 19–21): Over time, the carpenter's role has evolved, incorporating modern materials like plywood, chipboard, and metal, while still relying on timber as the primary material.

The 4 Core Tasks of a Carpenter (Page 1, Lines 10–18)

1. Reading & Interpreting Plans
Page 1, Lines 11–12

Working from construction drawings and performing necessary dimensional and material calculations.

2. Framing Page 1, Lines 13–14

Building the structural skeleton of a building, including floors, walls, and roofs.

3. Interior & Exterior Finishing Page 1, Lines 15–16

Installing items such as door frames, windows, cabinets, staircases, and trim.

4. Formwork (Shuttering) Page 1, Lines 17–18

Constructing temporary molds (shuttering) into which concrete is poured.

2. Timber & Key Properties of Good Timber

Engineering definition and the 5 quality indicators of sound timber

PDF Page 1 (Lines 22–25) & Page 2 (Lines 1–13)
Definition of Timber (Page 1, Lines 22–25)

Timber refers to wood that has been processed and is suitable for building, carpentry, or other engineering purposes. It is the fundamental material in carpentry, prized for its strength, workability, and low heat conductivity.

5 Key Properties of Good Timber (Page 2, Lines 1–11):

1. Strength & Durability Pg 2, L3–4

Strong enough for its intended use and resistant to decay.

2. Appearance Pg 2, L5–6

Should have a uniform color, a shining fresh-cut surface, and a sweet smell.

3. Soundness Pg 2, L7–8

Produces a sonorous sound when struck, indicating it is free from internal defects.

4. Density Pg 2, L9

Generally, heavier timber is considered stronger.

5. Texture Pg 2, L10

Should have a uniform and close grain.

Standard commercial shapes and exact millimeter dimensions after log conversion

PDF Page 2 • Lines 14–35

What is Conversion? (Page 2, Lines 15–17): After a tree is felled, the log is "converted" or cut down into various marketable, standard sizes and shapes. This process prepares the raw timber for different uses in construction and carpentry.

1. Log Pg 2, L19–20

The trunk of a felled tree with its branches removed.

Dim: Raw, unprocessed form
2. Balk Pg 2, L21–23

Roughly square-shaped piece, obtained after removing bark and sapwood from a log.

Cross-section > 50mm × 50mm; Length > 200mm
3. Plank Pg 2, L24–25

A piece of timber that is wider than it is thick.

Thickness < 50mm; Breadth > 50mm
4. Batten Pg 2, L26–27

A rectangular piece of timber, narrower and thinner than a plank.

Thickness: 50–100mm; Breadth: 125–175mm
5. Beam Pg 2, L28–29

A large, horizontal timber piece used as a primary structural element.

Large cross-section; varies by load
6. Board Pg 2, L30–31

A thin, wide piece of sawn timber.

Thickness < 50mm; Breadth > 150mm
7. Deal Pg 2, L32–33

A converted form of a softwood log, generally rectangular in cross-section.

Thickness: 50–100mm; Breadth up to 250mm
8. Pole / Spar Pg 2, L34–35

A long, round-shaped piece of timber.

Maximum diameter approx. 200mm

4. Types of Wood: Hardwood and Softwood

Botanical classification, cellular sap flow structure, and engineering comparison

PDF Page 3 • Lines 1–20

Crucial Classification Rule (Page 3, Lines 2–4): Timber is primarily classified into hardwood and softwood based on the botanical type of tree and its resulting cellular structure, not necessarily the physical hardness of the wood.

Hardwood (Deciduous) Oak, Maple, Teak
Softwood (Coniferous) Pine, Fir, Spruce
Feature Hardwood (Page 3, Lines 6–20) Softwood (Page 3, Lines 6–20)
1. Tree Type Lines 6–8 Comes from deciduous (broad-leaved) trees that lose their leaves annually, e.g., oak, maple, teak. Comes from coniferous (needle-bearing) trees that are typically evergreen, e.g., pine, fir, spruce.
2. Structure Lines 9–11 Has a complex structure with pores or vessels for sap flow; grains are typically closed and prominently visible. Has a simpler structure; sap circulates through wood fibers; grains are typically open and light.
3. Density & Weight Lines 12–13 Generally more dense, heavier, and harder. Generally less dense, lighter, and softer.
4. Workability Lines 14–16 Often more difficult to work with due to its density; can be harder on tools. Easier to work with because of its lower density and simpler composition.
5. Cost & Availability Lines 17–18 Generally more expensive and less readily available. More readily available and less expensive.
6. Common Uses Lines 19–20 Cabinetry, fine furniture, decorative woodwork, flooring. Building construction, framing, roofing, and general structural work.

5. Seasoning of Wood

Controlled moisture reduction: Natural (Air) vs. Artificial (Kiln, Water, Boiling)

PDF Page 3 (Lines 21–35) & Page 4 (Lines 1–26)

Definition & Problem with Green Timber (Page 3, Lines 22–25): Seasoning is the controlled process of reducing the moisture content of freshly cut (green) timber. This is a critical step as green timber is heavy, weak, prone to decay, and will shrink and warp as it dries.

1. Natural (Air) Seasoning

Pg 3, L26–35
  • • Process: Sawn timber stacked under a covered shed allowing free air circulation around each piece.
  • • Duration: Takes several months to years (depends on climate & thickness).
  • • Moisture Content: Reduces moisture to 12–15%.
  • • Advantages: Simple, inexpensive, superior for certain uses.
  • • Disadvantages: Very slow process; requires a large storage area.

2. Artificial: Kiln Seasoning

Pg 4, L1–10
  • • Process: Timber placed in a large oven (kiln) where heated air, steam, or dehumidified air is circulated to rapidly and uniformly remove moisture.
  • • Duration: Completed in about 7 days.
  • • Advantages: Fast, precise control over final moisture content, seasons to very low moisture levels.
  • • Disadvantages: More expensive; requires specialized equipment.

3. Water & Boiling Methods

Pg 4, L12–15
  • • Water Seasoning (Line 13): Logs are immersed in water to leach out sap, then dried in the air.
  • • Boiling (Lines 14–15): Exposing wood to boiling water or steam, which is quick but expensive.

5 Core Objectives of Seasoning (Page 4, Lines 16–26)

1. Reduce Shrinkage & Warping Prevents wood from changing shape after it is used in a structure.
2. Increase Strength & Workability Makes wood easier to cut and less prone to decay and insect attack.
3. Reduce Weight Makes the timber lighter and easier to handle.
4. Suitable for Finishing Allows for better paint and polish adhesion.
5. Prevent Splitting & Decay Reduces the risk of splitting and biological fungal decay.

6. Carpentry Joints

Functional classifications and all 9 traditional woodworking joints with SVG schematics

PDF Page 4 (Lines 27–30) & Page 5 (Lines 1–35)
Joint Selection (Pg 4, L27–30)

A carpentry joint connects two or more pieces of wood. Choice depends on required strength, appearance, and the type of stress the joint will endure.

Classification A1 (Pg 5, L3–4)

1. Widening Joints (Edge Joints)

Used to join boards side-by-side to create a wider panel (e.g., Tongue and Groove).

Classification A2 (Pg 5, L5–6)

2. Corner Joints

Used to join two pieces at an angle, typically at the corners of frames or boxes (e.g., Butt, Miter, Dovetail).

Classification A3 (Pg 5, L7–8)

3. Framing Joints

Used to connect the structural members of a frame (e.g., Mortise and Tenon).

B. Common Types of Joints — Visual Schematics & Typical Uses (Page 5, Lines 10–35)

1. Butt Joint Pg 5, L12–13

Description: The simplest joint; the end of one piece is butted against another.

Use: Basic framing, where strength is not a primary concern.
2. Miter Joint Pg 5, L14–15

Description: Similar to a butt joint, but both pieces are cut at a 15° angle (verbatim PDF text; 45° in standard practice) to form a neat corner.

Use: Picture frames, window and door casings.
3. Lap (Half-Lap) Joint Pg 5, L16–18

Description: One piece overlaps another. In a Half-Lap Joint, half of each piece's thickness is removed so they fit flush.

Use: Cabinetry, bracing, and furniture frames.
4. Dado Joint Pg 5, L19–20

Description: A square slot is cut across the grain of one piece for another piece to fit into.

Use: Bookshelves, cabinets, and drawer dividers.
5. Tongue and Groove Pg 5, L21–23

Description: A groove is cut along the edge of one board, and a matching tongue (ridge) is cut on the edge of the other.

Use: Flooring, paneling, and tabletops.
6. Box (Finger) Joint Pg 5, L24–25

Description: A series of interlocking, square "fingers" are cut at the ends of two boards.

Use: Corners of boxes, drawers, and chests.
7. Dovetail Joint Pg 5, L26–28

Description: A form of box joint where the fingers are cut at an angle, creating a "dovetail" shape that locks together.

Use: High-quality drawers, cabinets, and furniture; known for exceptional strength.
8. Mortise and Tenon Pg 5, L29–31

Description: A mortise (hole or slot) is cut into one piece, and a matching tenon (tongue) is cut on the end of the other to fit snugly inside.

Use: Door and window frames, furniture, timber framing; one of the strongest traditional joints.
9. Dowelled Joint Pg 5, L32–34

Description: Wooden dowels (pins) are inserted into corresponding holes drilled into both pieces for alignment and mechanical strength.

Use: A simpler alternative to mortise and tenon for furniture and cabinet construction.
