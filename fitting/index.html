1. Introduction to Fittings & Its Importance

Fundamental workshop practice, tolerances, and the 5 engineering pillars of fitting

PDF Page 1 • Lines 4–31

Introduction to Fittings (Page 1, Lines 5–14): Fitting is a fundamental workshop practice that involves the assembly of mechanical components to form a functional machine or structure. It is the art of manually cutting, shaping, and finishing metal parts to precise dimensions so that they can be joined together. Fitting work is primarily performed on a workbench using hand tools, but it also involves the use of power tools for efficiency and accuracy.

The Fitter's Role & Tolerances (Page 1, Lines 11–14): A fitter's job is to ensure that components fit together with the correct tolerances, allowing for movement, securing parts, or creating a tight seal as required. In engineering and manufacturing, fitting is the crucial link between design and the final product (Lines 16–17).

5 Pillars of Importance of Fittings (Page 1, Lines 18–31)

1. Precision & Accuracy Page 1, Lines 18–20

Ensures manufactured parts meet exact dimensions specified in the design, guaranteeing interchangeability and proper function.

2. Assembly & Functionality Page 1, Lines 21–23

Without precise fitting, components cannot be assembled correctly. Poor fitting leads to machine failure, excessive wear, and inefficiency.

3. Quality Control Page 1, Lines 24–25

Essential for checking and correcting minor dimensional errors in castings or machined parts.

4. Maintenance & Repair Page 1, Lines 26–28

Vital for repairing worn-out parts or replacing components on-site, keeping machinery operational.

5. Versatility Page 1, Lines 29–31

Required in almost every sector: automotive, aerospace, shipbuilding, and construction.

2. Fitting Operations & Workshop Tools

All 8 core manual & machine fitting operations with tool classifications

PDF Page 2 (Lines 1–30) & Page 3 (Lines 1–20)

1. Job Holding

Page 2, Lines 4–10

The workpiece must be held securely to perform operations safely and accurately. Common tools include:

  • • Bench Vice: The most common tool, used for filing, sawing, and chipping.
  • • V-Block & Clamps: Specifically used for holding round or cylindrical jobs.
  • • Angle Plate: Used to hold workpieces at an exact 90° angle.
  • • C-Clamps: Used for holding jobs on a machine table.

2. Filing

Page 2, Lines 11–14

A metal removal process using a hardened steel file to remove burrs, smoothen surfaces, and bring a workpiece to its final shape and size.

Primary File Classifications:
1. By Shape (flat, round, half-round, triangular, square)
2. By Cut Grade: Rough • Bastard • Second Cut • Smooth
3. By Length

3. Measuring

Page 2, Lines 15–23

Determining the dimensions of a workpiece to ensure it meets specifications:

  • • Steel Rule: For basic linear measurements.
  • • Outside/Inside Calipers: For transferring and comparing dimensions.
  • • Vernier Caliper: For precise linear measurements and internal/external diameters.
  • • Micrometer: For very high-precision measurements of thickness and diameters.

4. Marking (First Step)

Pg 2, L24–30 & Pg 3, L1–4

Transferring dimensions and outlines from a drawing onto the workpiece. It is the very first step in any fitting operation.

  • • Surface Plate: A true flat surface used as a reference.
  • • Scriber: A sharp-pointed tool used for drawing lines.
  • • Vernier Height Gauge: Used to mark precise heights from a surface plate.
  • • Dividers: Used for marking arcs, circles, and transferring distances.
  • • Center Punch: Creates an indent at the center of a hole or intersection of lines to guide a drill.

5. Sawing

Page 3, Lines 5–8

The process of cutting metal manually using a hacksaw. Used for cutting bars, rods, and pipes to a rough length. Consists of a frame and a blade with teeth. The blade must be chosen based on the material being cut.

6. Chipping

Page 3, Lines 9–12

Removing excess metal using a chisel and a hammer. Used for roughing out surfaces, cutting off metal, and removing sharp edges. The chisel is held at a specific angle and struck with a ball-peen hammer.

7. Drilling

Page 3, Lines 13–15

The process of creating a circular hole in a workpiece using a drilling machine. The drill bit is rotated and fed into the workpiece, removing material in the form of chips.

8. Reaming

Page 3, Lines 16–20

A finishing operation that follows drilling. A reamer (multi-edged cutting tool) slightly enlarges and finishes a pre-drilled hole to a very precise diameter and superior surface finish for dowel pins or shafts.

3. Screw Threads: Nomenclature & Standard Types

Helical ridge geometry, 9 nomenclature parameters, and Metric / BSW / BSP / TPI standards

PDF Page 3 (Lines 21–32) & Page 4 (Lines 1–23)

Definition of a Thread (Page 3, Lines 22–23): A thread is a helical ridge of uniform section formed on the outside (external thread) or inside (internal thread) of a cylinder or cone.

Technical Schematic: Screw Thread Nomenclature (Axial Plane Profile)
1. Major (Nominal) DiameterPg 3, L24–25

The largest diameter of an external thread (or the smallest diameter of an internal thread).

2. Minor (Core) DiameterPg 3, L26–27

The smallest diameter of an external thread (or the largest diameter of an internal thread).

3. Pitch Diameter (Critical Fit)Pg 3, L28–30

Imaginary cylinder diameter where thread width and space width are equal. Most critical dimension for thread fit.

4. Pitch (P)Pg 3, L31–32

Distance measured parallel to the thread axis from a point on one thread to the corresponding point on the adjacent thread.

5. Lead (L)Pg 4, L1–3

Distance a screw advances in one complete revolution. Single-start: L = P. Multi-start: L = P × Starts.

6. Flank, Crest & RootPg 4, L4–6

Flank: Straight side of thread. Crest: Top surface of thread. Root: Bottom surface of thread.

Different Types of Standard Threads (Page 4, Lines 9–23)

Thread Standard Angle & Profile Designation & Key Application (PDF Page 4, Lines 10–23)
1. Metric Thread 60° Equilateral Triangle Most common globally. Nominal diameter and pitch are given in millimeters (e.g., M10 × 1.5 = 10mm diameter, 1.5mm pitch).
2. BSW (British Standard Whitworth) 55° Angle (Rounded Crests & Roots) Size designated by nominal diameter in inches (e.g., 1/2" BSW). Pitch is given in TPI (Threads Per Inch).
3. BSP (British Standard Pipe) 55° Pipe Thread Angle Typically tapered on the male (BSPT) and parallel on the female (BSPP). Used for sealing fluid connections.
4. TPI (Threads Per Inch) TPI = 1 / Pitch (Reciprocal) Number of threads counted along the length of one inch. Defines standard fasteners in the Imperial system (BSW and UNC).

4. Tapping, Tap Drill Size Calculation & Dieing

Internal threading (Tap), External threading (Die & Stock), and exact tap drill formulas

PDF Page 4 (Lines 24–33) & Page 5 (Lines 1–16)

A. Tapping (Internal Threading)

Pg 4, L24–33 & Pg 5, L1–5

Tapping is the process of cutting an internal thread (within a hole) using a tool called a tap. Before tapping, a hole of a specific diameter must be drilled, known as the tap drill size.

If Tap Drill is Too Small: The tap will break inside the hole.
If Tap Drill is Too Large: The resulting threads will be shallow and weak.
1. Metric Threads (60°) Formula (Page 4, Lines 31–33)
Tap Drill Size (mm) = Nominal Diameter (mm) − Pitch (mm)

PDF Worked Example: For an M10 × 1.5 thread, Tap Drill = 10 − 1.5 = 8.5 mm.

2. BSW / UNC Threads (Inch) Formula (Page 5, Lines 1–5)
Tap Drill Size (Inch) = Nominal Diameter (Inch) − (1 / TPI)

PDF Worked Example: For a 1/2" BSW thread (12 TPI), Tap Drill = 0.5 − (1/12) = 0.5 − 0.0833 = 0.4167 inches (≈ 27/64" drill).

B. Dieing (External Thread Cutting)

Page 5, Lines 6–16
  • • Definition: Cutting an external thread on a cylindrical rod using a hardened steel tool called a die (which has a central hole with internal teeth).
  • • Work Holding & Lubrication: Rod is held securely in a vice, often with a V-block to protect it. Cutting oil is applied for lubrication.
  • • Stock & Starting: The die is fitted into a stock (a holder), placed squarely on the end of the rod, and turned with gentle pressure to start the cut.
  • • Chip Breaking: After a few threads are cut, the die is reversed slightly to break the chips, then advanced forward again.

5. Twist Drill & Its Nomenclature

Complete geometry of the twist drill, shank types, and cutting point angles (118° vs 135°)

PDF Page 5 • Lines 17–34
Technical Diagram: Twist Drill Geometry & Point Angles
1. PointPage 5, Line 19

The conical cutting end of the twist drill.

2. BodyPage 5, Line 20

The main cylindrical part extending from the flutes to the shank.

3. ShankPage 5, Lines 21–22

The driving end of the drill: either straight (held in a chuck) or tapered (Morse taper, held directly in the spindle).

4. FlutesPage 5, Lines 23–25

Helical grooves running along the body. They form the cutting edges, allow chips to escape, and permit cutting fluid to reach the point.

5. Lands (Margin)Page 5, Lines 26–27

The narrow cylindrical strips on the periphery of the drill body that guide the drill in the hole.

6. FacePage 5, Line 28

The surface of the flute on which the chip slides.

7. Lip (Cutting Edge)Page 5, Line 29

The leading edge of the drill point that performs the cutting action.

8. Lip Clearance AnglePage 5, Lines 30–31

Angle between the lip and a plane perpendicular to the drill axis. Provides clearance for the cutting edge.

9. Point Angle (118° / 135°)Page 5, Lines 32–34

Angle between the two cutting lips: typically 118° for general-purpose drilling in steel and 135° for harder materials.

6. Power Tools — Upright Drilling Machine

Construction components, multi-purpose operations, work holding, and shop safety rules

PDF Page 6 • Lines 1–28
Labeled Schematic: Upright Drilling Machine

7 Core Construction Components (Page 6, Lines 4–18):

1. Base (Lines 5–6): Heavy, rigid base supports the entire machine and is bolted to the floor to prevent vibration.
2. Column / Pillar (Lines 7–8): A vertical cylindrical column rising from the base that supports all other components.
3. Table (Lines 9–11): Mounted on the column; can be raised, lowered, and sometimes tilted. Features T-slots to clamp workpieces and vises securely.
4. Head (Lines 12–13): Upper part of the machine housing the drive mechanism, motor, and spindle.
5. Spindle (Lines 14–15): Rotating shaft holding the chuck/tool. Driven by a motor via a belt or gear drive.
6. Drill Chuck & 7. Feed Mechanism (Lines 16–18): Drill Chuck attaches to spindle to hold the drill bit. Feed Mechanism (hand lever or automatic) moves spindle & drill down into workpiece.
Operations (Pg 6, L19–21)
Besides simple drilling, it performs reaming, tapping (using a tapping attachment), countersinking, and counterboring.
Work Holding (Pg 6, L22–24)
A machine vice is the most common method. For larger parts, T-bolts and clamps are used directly on the table.
Safety Rules (Pg 6, L25–28)
Always wear safety glasses, secure workpiece, ensure drill bit is sharp and tightened, and never wear loose clothing or jewelry near the rotating spindle.
